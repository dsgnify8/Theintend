Introduction

Before You Start

Almost everything written about gut health falls into one of two piles. The first is frightening and vague: your gut is the root of all disease, and here is a protocol. The second is so cautious it says nothing you can act on.

This book tries to sit in the useful middle. Part One explains the machinery, because you cannot fix what you do not understand. Part Two covers the connections that are genuinely established, and is honest about which ones are still speculation. Part Three is for when something is actually wrong. Part Four is what to do. Part Five is how to tell whether it worked.

You will notice that the practical advice is short. That is not because there is nothing to say. It is because the interventions that reliably work are few, and most of what fills the shelves is expensive noise.

A promise about food

This book will not hand you a list of foods to fear. Restriction is the most common way gut advice does harm, and a smaller diet is very often a less healthy gut.

Where removing something is genuinely useful, it is time-limited and structured, and it is followed by putting it back.

Understand

The system, honestly, including the parts that get exaggerated.

Change

A small number of habits, applied for long enough to judge.

Notice

Specific, trackable signs. Not a feeling that things are vaguely better.

When to stop reading and see a doctor

Unexplained weight loss · blood in the stool, or black stools · persistent vomiting · difficulty swallowing · severe abdominal pain · symptoms that wake you at night · anaemia · persistent diarrhoea · a family history of inflammatory bowel disease or bowel cancer. None of these are things to manage with diet while you wait and see.

Chapter One

What Gut Health Actually Means

It is not a feeling, and it is not a product category. It is four measurable things working at once.

10
Chapter One · What It Means

“Gut health” has become one of those phrases that means everything and therefore nothing. It is worth being precise, because precision is what lets you tell whether anything is improving.

A healthy gut is doing four separate jobs well at the same time. When people say their gut is bad, they usually mean one of these four has slipped, and knowing which one changes what you should do about it.

Digestion

breaking things down

Enough chewing, enough stomach acid, enough enzymes from the pancreas, enough bile from the liver. If this fails you get fullness, belching, undigested food, and nutrients that never make it in.

Absorption

taking things up

A working intestinal surface that pulls nutrients across into the blood. If this fails you can eat well and still be deficient. Fatigue, low ferritin and low B12 often start here.

Motility

moving things along

A coordinated set of muscular waves that keep contents travelling at the right speed. Too slow gives constipation and fermentation. Too fast gives urgency and poor absorption.

The barrier and its residents

deciding what gets in

A mucus layer, a single cell-thick wall with sealed joins, an immune system behind it, and trillions of microbes on top. This is the part that talks to the rest of your body.

Why the distinction matters

Someone with slow motility and someone with an inflamed barrier both say “I feel bloated”. But one needs fibre, hydration and movement, while the other may get worse on exactly that. Nearly every failed gut protocol comes from treating the wrong one of these four.

11

Chapter Two

The Journey

One tube, nine metres, and roughly a day and a half. Here is what happens at each stop.

12
Chapter Two · The Journey

Digestion is a relay. Each stage prepares the next, and a failure early on shows up as a symptom much further down, which is why the place it hurts is so often not the place with the problem.

The mouth. Chewing is the only mechanical step you consciously control, and most people skip it. Saliva begins breaking down starch and signals the stomach to get ready. Food swallowed in lumps arrives as work the stomach was not expecting.

The stomach. Acid strong enough to be genuinely corrosive, around pH 1.5 to 3.5, unfolds proteins and sterilises much of what arrives. It also triggers the release of enzymes downstream. Low acid, which becomes more common with age and with long-term acid-suppressing medication, is a quiet cause of bloating and poor iron and B12 absorption.

The small intestine. Around seven metres of it, and where almost all absorption happens. Bile from the liver emulsifies fat, enzymes from the pancreas finish the job, and the nutrients cross the wall. This section is meant to be relatively sparsely populated by bacteria, a detail that matters enormously in Chapter Thirteen.

The colon. Water and minerals are reclaimed, and the residue meets the densest microbial population on earth. What happens here is not waste disposal. It is fermentation, and it produces compounds your body genuinely depends on.

13
Chapter Two · The Journey

Where it usually goes wrong

Most digestive complaints trace to one of a short list: too little chewing, too little acid, too few enzymes, an imbalance in the microbes, inflammation of the wall, motility running at the wrong speed, or a nervous system that never left alert mode long enough to digest anything.

Notice how few of those are about which foods you ate. Food matters, and Part Four is largely about it, but the machinery matters first. Someone eating an immaculate diet in a permanent rush, chewing four times and swallowing, will still feel unwell.

Transit time, the total journey from mouth to exit, is normally somewhere between twenty-four and seventy-two hours. Much faster and you are not absorbing properly. Much slower and material sits fermenting, water keeps being reclaimed, and stools harden.

You can measure your own transit time, and it is one of the more useful numbers you will ever collect about yourself. Eat a handful of whole sweetcorn or a tablespoon of undigestible seeds, note the time, and note when they appear. That is your transit.

The housekeeping wave

Between meals, when the small intestine is empty, a slow wave of contraction sweeps from the stomach downwards roughly every ninety minutes. Its job is to clear residue and bacteria out of the small intestine and into the colon. It only runs when you are not eating. Continuous grazing, and snacking late into the evening, switches it off. This is one of the few genuinely good arguments for leaving gaps between meals, and it has nothing to do with calories.

Chapter Three

The Microbiome, Without the Myths

An ecosystem, not a battlefield. The useful questions are about balance and diversity, not good and evil.

15
Chapter Three · The Microbiome

Your large intestine holds something in the order of thirty to forty trillion microorganisms. They are not passengers. They ferment the fibre you cannot digest, synthesise vitamins including K and several B vitamins, train your immune system to tell friend from threat, occupy space that would otherwise be available to pathogens, and produce chemicals that reach your brain.

The framing that does the most damage is good bacteria versus bad bacteria. Almost no gut microbe is simply one or the other. Many organisms that cause disease in the wrong place or the wrong quantity are entirely benign, even useful, in balance. What predicts health is not the presence or absence of particular species but diversity: how many different kinds are present, and how evenly.

A diverse community is resilient. Lose one member to a course of antibiotics and another covers the function. A community dominated by a handful of species is brittle, and behaves unpredictably when disturbed.

Two numbers worth correcting

You have probably read that microbes outnumber your own cells ten to one. The best current estimate is closer to one to one.

And the microbiome is often described as weighing two kilograms. Careful estimates put it nearer two hundred grams.

Neither correction makes it less important. But a field that repeats its own folklore is a field to read carefully.

Different shapes, different jobs. Resilience comes from the variety, not from any one species.

16
Chapter Three · The Microbiome

What they make for you

The single most important thing your gut bacteria do is ferment fibre into short-chain fatty acids. If you remember one mechanism from this book, make it this one, because it explains why fibre keeps appearing in every chapter.

Butyrate is the primary fuel for the cells lining your colon. They feed on what your bacteria make.

What damages diversity

A diet low in plant variety, above all. Then repeated courses of antibiotics, which can reduce diversity for months. Then chronic stress, poor sleep, very low fibre intake, excessive alcohol, and long-term use of some medications including acid suppressants. Smoking. And prolonged restrictive dieting, which is the one people undertake believing it will help.

What rebuilds it

Plant variety, again above all. Fermented foods. Adequate fibre, increased gradually. Time outdoors and contact with soil and animals. Sleep. And patience: composition begins shifting within days of a dietary change, but meaningful, stable change in diversity is a matter of months.

17
Chapter Three · The Microbiome

Probiotics: an honest answer

Probiotics work, in specific situations, for specific strains. That sentence contains the whole difficulty.

The evidence is reasonable for reducing antibiotic-associated diarrhoea, for some presentations of IBS, for certain infectious diarrhoeas particularly in children, and as an adjunct in ulcerative colitis under medical supervision. Outside those, the case for routine daily use in a healthy person is weak.

Crucially, benefit is strain-specific, not species-specific and certainly not product-specific. A trial showing that one particular strain helped one particular condition tells you almost nothing about the tub on the shelf with a different strain in it. The number of billions on the label is close to meaningless without knowing which organism, and whether that organism has been studied for what you have.

For most people in reasonable health, a varied high-fibre diet with some fermented food does more for the microbiome than a supplement, costs less, and does not stop working when you stop buying it.

Commercial microbiome testing

The tests are real, in that they genuinely sequence what is in your sample. The problem is interpretation. We do not yet have a validated definition of an optimal microbiome, results vary substantially depending on where in a stool the sample came from and how it was handled, and the dietary advice generated from them is usually the same advice this book gives for free. Interesting. Rarely actionable.

If you have just finished antibiotics

This is one of the clearest cases for acting. Eat a deliberately wide range of plants for the following month, include fermented foods daily if you tolerate them, and be patient: diversity typically takes weeks to months to recover, and a small number of species may not return. Do not let that put you off necessary antibiotics. Let it inform what you do afterwards.

Chapter Four

The Wall

A single layer of cells stands between what you swallowed and your bloodstream. Everything depends on how well it is sealed.

19
Chapter Four · The Wall

Between the contents of your intestine and the inside of your body there is a barrier one cell thick. That is all. Those cells are joined by protein seals called tight junctions, covered by a mucus layer, and backed by the densest concentration of immune tissue you own.

Mucus, then a single layer of sealed cells, then villi to multiply the surface, then the immune system waiting behind.

What the barrier is for

It has to do two contradictory things at once: absorb nutrients efficiently, and refuse entry to bacteria, toxins and partly digested proteins. The tight junctions are not fixed shut. They open and close in a regulated way, all the time. That regulation is the point.

Villi and surface area

The intestinal lining is folded, then covered in finger-like villi, then those are covered in microvilli. The often-repeated claim that this gives you the surface area of a tennis court is wrong; careful measurement puts it around thirty to forty square metres. Still the largest surface where your body meets the outside world.

20
Chapter Four · The Wall

So is “leaky gut” real?

Yes and no, and the distinction matters more here than almost anywhere else in this book.

Increased intestinal permeability is real. It is a measurable phenomenon, it can be quantified in research settings, and it is genuinely observed in coeliac disease, in inflammatory bowel disease, in some metabolic conditions, after significant burns or trauma, and with heavy alcohol use. The biology is not in dispute.

“Leaky gut syndrome” as sold to the public is a different thing. The claim that a permeable barrier is the hidden cause of most chronic illness, diagnosable by symptom questionnaire and treatable by a supplement bundle, is not supported. In many conditions where permeability is increased, it appears to be a consequence of the disease rather than its cause.

The practical position is straightforward. The barrier is real and worth looking after. In an otherwise healthy person, it repairs itself when the things damaging it are removed, and it is fed by exactly what the rest of this book recommends.

What actually supports the barrier

Butyrate from fermented fibre, which is the main fuel for the cells that form the barrier.

Adequate protein. Zinc and vitamin A where deficient. Sleep. Moderate alcohol. Not smoking. And treating any underlying inflammation rather than papering over it.

Notice that none of it is exotic.

A useful rule for this whole subject

When a mechanism is real but a marketing claim is built on top of it, the tell is the leap from “this exists” to “this explains your symptoms and here is what to buy”. Increased permeability exists. Whether it explains what you are feeling is a separate question, and usually an unanswered one.

The Connections

Why an organ that handles food ends up shaping your mood, your skin and your defences.

Chapter Five

The Gut and the Mind

The most oversold relationship in modern health, and also one of the most real. Both things are true.

23
Chapter Five · The Gut and the Mind

Start with the anatomy, because it settles the argument before the marketing gets involved.

Your gut wall contains its own nervous system, the enteric nervous system, with something in the region of five hundred million neurons. It can coordinate digestion entirely on its own, without instruction from the brain. No other organ has anything comparable. This is what people are pointing at when they call the gut a second brain, and for once the phrase is not an exaggeration.

It is connected to the brain by the vagus nerve. Here is the detail that reframes everything: roughly nine out of ten of the fibres in that nerve carry information upward, from gut to brain, not downward. The traffic is overwhelmingly your gut reporting to your head.

Alongside the nerve, three other channels run constantly: hormones released by gut cells in response to food, immune signalling from the tissue behind the barrier, and metabolites made by your bacteria that enter the bloodstream. Four routes, running in both directions, all day.

24
Chapter Five · The Gut and the Mind

What this does and does not mean

This is where a good book has to be more careful than a popular one, so let us separate what is established from what is hopeful.

Well established

Stress measurably changes digestion: motility, secretion, blood flow and sensitivity.

Digestive disorders are strongly associated with anxiety and low mood, in both directions.

Gut bacteria produce neuroactive compounds, and germ-free animals show altered stress behaviour.

The vagus nerve carries gut signals that influence brain regions involved in mood and threat.

Not established

That changing your microbiome alone treats depression or anxiety in humans.

That a particular probiotic will reliably improve your mood.

That gut bacteria cause neurological disease.

That any commercially available test can tell you your mood is caused by your gut.

The serotonin claim, corrected

You will read everywhere that ninety to ninety-five per cent of your serotonin is made in your gut, usually followed by the implication that your gut therefore controls your mood. The first half is true. The second half does not follow: serotonin made in the gut does not cross the blood-brain barrier. It is doing local work there, regulating motility and secretion, and it is genuinely important for that. It is not the serotonin your antidepressant is aimed at. Anyone who runs those two facts together is not being careful with you.

What is fair to say

Your gut is in constant conversation with your brain, and that conversation shapes how you feel more than most people realise. But the honest direction of the strongest evidence runs the way that is least often advertised: your mental state is one of the most powerful influences on your digestion. Treating your stress is a gut intervention.

25
Chapter Five · The Gut and the Mind

Why your stomach knows first

Everyone has felt this. The sinking sensation before bad news. Nausea before an exam. The stomach that closes when you are grieving and will not open for days. Urgency before something you are dreading.

This is not metaphor and it is not weakness. It is the sympathetic nervous system doing precisely what it evolved to do. Under perceived threat, blood is diverted away from the digestive tract toward muscle. Secretion of acid and enzymes falls. Motility in the upper gut slows while the lower gut speeds up, which is a coherent strategy if you are about to run, and miserable if you are sitting in a meeting.

At the same time, visceral sensitivity increases. The same volume of gas that you would not notice on a calm day registers as pain. This is the central mechanism in IBS, and it explains something that otherwise makes no sense: two people can have identical amounts of gas and only one is in agony.

Which is why a chapter that appears to be about feelings belongs in a book about digestion, and why the breathing exercise in Part Four is not a soft extra. It is one of the few interventions here that works within a minute.

If your symptoms are worse in stressful periods

That is diagnostic information, not something to be embarrassed about. It does not mean your symptoms are imaginary; visceral hypersensitivity is a physical phenomenon with measurable thresholds. It means that the highest-yield intervention available to you may not be dietary at all. Gut-directed hypnotherapy and cognitive behavioural therapy have some of the strongest evidence bases in all of IBS management, better than most diets.

Chapter Six

The Gut and the Immune System

The best-evidenced connection in this book, and the reason gut health is not a niche concern.

27
Chapter Six · Gut and Immunity

Around seventy per cent of your immune tissue is arranged along the gut. Once you know that, the whole subject stops looking like a digestive speciality and starts looking like general health.

The reason is logistical. The gut is the largest surface where your body meets the outside world, and everything you swallow arrives at it. Your immune system has to make an extraordinarily difficult judgement there, thousands of times a day: tolerate this harmless food protein, tolerate this beneficial microbe, attack this pathogen. Getting that wrong in one direction gives infection. Getting it wrong in the other gives food allergy and autoimmunity.

Your microbes are directly involved in teaching it. They help train regulatory immune cells, the ones whose job is to say stand down, this is fine. This is not a fringe theory; it is one of the better-supported findings in the field, and it is why early-life microbial exposure keeps appearing in allergy research.

The practical version

Butyrate, made by your bacteria from fibre, is directly involved in maintaining the barrier and in supporting regulatory immune cells. Low fibre means less butyrate, which means a less well-fed barrier and less of the signalling that keeps immune responses proportionate. This is the mechanism connecting a low-plant diet to chronic low-grade inflammation, and it is the same mechanism running through every other chapter in this part.

Chapter Seven

The Gut and the Skin

Real, complicated, and frequently oversold. Worth understanding precisely so you can spot the exaggerations.

29
Chapter Seven · Gut and Skin

Skin and gut have a great deal in common. Both are barriers. Both carry their own microbial communities. Both are policed by immune tissue. And both are exquisitely responsive to inflammation elsewhere in the body.

The evidence for a connection is strongest in rosacea, where the association with small intestinal bacterial overgrowth is well documented and treatment of the overgrowth has produced meaningful skin improvement in trials. It is reasonable in eczema, particularly in early life, where microbial exposure and immune training appear to matter. It is weakest, and most oversold, in acne, where the association is real but the mechanism is tangled up with insulin, androgens and inflammation rather than being simply a gut story.

The plausible pathways are the same ones from the last two chapters: inflammatory signals produced in the gut circulate; barrier disruption alters immune signalling; and microbial metabolites influence inflammation systemically. None of this is mysterious. It is also not a licence for the claim that clearing your gut clears your skin.

What is fair to say: if you have a skin condition and also have persistent digestive symptoms, the two may well be connected, and investigating the gut is reasonable. If your digestion is genuinely fine, gut protocols are an unlikely route to better skin.

Where this gets harmful

Skin is visible, which makes it emotionally loaded, which makes people willing to try severe restriction. Cutting dairy, gluten, sugar and nightshades simultaneously to fix your face is the fastest route to a narrowed diet, a less diverse microbiome, and a worse relationship with eating. If you are going to test a food, test one, and read Chapter Twenty on how to do it properly.

The honest summary

Improving your gut may improve your skin, particularly in rosacea and where digestive symptoms coexist. It is not a substitute for dermatological treatment, and the timeline is months rather than weeks. Both can be true at once.

Chapter Eight

The Gut and Your Hormones

A specific, well-established mechanism, and one of the clearest reasons gut health is not only a digestive matter.

31
Chapter Eight · Gut and Hormones

Hormonal balance is not only about how much you produce. It is equally about how efficiently you clear what you have finished with, and a surprising amount of that job belongs to your gut.

Oestrogen, once it has done its work, travels to the liver where it is chemically packaged so it can be excreted. That package leaves in bile, enters the intestine, and is meant to leave the body.

A subset of gut bacteria carry an enzyme that can unwrap it. Freed, the oestrogen returns to its active form and is reabsorbed instead of eliminated. This collection of bacteria has a name: the estrobolome.

In balance, this recycling is modest and normal. When the microbiome is disrupted, or when transit is slow enough to leave material sitting in the colon, recirculation can rise. The result is a higher circulating oestrogen load with the ovaries producing nothing extra. Symptoms that follow, heavier bleeding, breast tenderness, worsening premenstrual weeks, are often read as an ovarian problem when part of the story is a clearance one.

Why constipation is a hormonal issue

This is the clearest practical consequence in the chapter. Slow transit means more time for reabsorption.

A daily bowel movement is not a vanity metric. For anyone with oestrogen-related symptoms it is part of the treatment.

What supports clearance

Fibre, which binds packaged oestrogen and carries it out, and which feeds the bacteria that keep the community balanced. Cruciferous vegetables, which supply compounds supporting the liver’s processing pathways. Regular bowel movements. Moderate alcohol, since alcohol both raises circulating oestrogen and competes for the liver capacity needed to clear it. Nothing exotic, and nothing you have to buy.

What this chapter does not claim

You will see the estrobolome used to sell detox protocols and expensive panels. The underlying biology is genuinely well established. The products built on top of it are mostly not. The gap between a real mechanism and a validated treatment is where most of this industry lives.

Chapter Nine

Bloating, Properly Explained

The most common complaint in this book, and the most misinterpreted.

35
Chapter Nine · Bloating

Bloating is a sensation: fullness, tightness, pressure. Distension is a visible increase in abdominal size. They often occur together and they are not the same thing, and a great deal of confusion follows from treating them as one.

Almost everyone who bloats assumes it means either food intolerance or excess gas. Frequently it is neither. Studies measuring intestinal gas in people with severe bloating often find volumes no higher than in people without it. What differs is perception and, in some cases, an abnormal reflex where the abdominal wall relaxes and the diaphragm drops, pushing the belly outward.

That is why bloating so often tracks stress, and why it is often worst in the evening after a day of accumulated tension and food.

The common causes, roughly ordered

Constipation, by a distance. Then swallowed air, from eating quickly, talking while eating, fizzy drinks and chewing gum. Then altered motility. Then IBS and visceral hypersensitivity. Then hormonal fluctuation across the cycle. Then genuine carbohydrate malabsorption. Then, less often, SIBO. Rarely, something structural.

Before you remove a single food

1Fix constipation first. It is the most common cause and the most overlooked. If you are not passing a comfortable stool most days, that is where the answer probably is.
2Look at how fast you eat. Swallowed air is a genuine and unglamorous cause. Eating slowly, without talking through mouthfuls, changes it within days.
3Note when it happens. Immediately after eating points to the stomach or to air. Several hours later points to fermentation lower down. Worst in the evening regardless of food points to accumulation and to stress.
4Check the cycle. If bloating clusters in the week before your period, that is hormonal, and no elimination diet will touch it.
5Then, and only then, consider food. And test one thing, for a defined period, in the structured way set out in Chapter Twenty.

When bloating needs a doctor

New and persistent bloating in anyone over fifty. Bloating with weight loss, a change in bowel habit that lasts more than a few weeks, blood in the stool, or a family history of bowel or ovarian cancer. Persistent bloating that does not fluctuate at all. These are not diet problems.

Chapter Ten

Too Slow, Too Fast

Constipation and diarrhoea are not opposites. They are the same system running at the wrong speed.

37
Chapter Ten · Motility

When it is too slow

The colon’s job includes reclaiming water. The longer material sits there, the more water is taken back, and the harder and more difficult to pass it becomes. Constipation is usually a transit problem before it is a stool problem.

What helps: fibre increased gradually, with a matching increase in fluid, because fibre without water makes constipation worse. Daily movement, which stimulates motility directly. A consistent time on the toilet, ideally after a meal when the gastrocolic reflex is active. A footstool to raise the knees above the hips, which straightens the anorectal angle and is one of the cheapest interventions in this book. Magnesium, where appropriate. And not ignoring the urge, which blunts the reflex over time.

When it is too fast

Material passes before water and nutrients can be reclaimed. Beyond the immediate discomfort, prolonged fast transit means poor absorption and, over time, deficiencies.

What helps: identifying the driver first, because the treatment differs entirely. Infection, medication side effects, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, bile acid malabsorption and IBS all present this way and none respond to the same thing. Soluble fibre such as oats or psyllium can help form stool. Rehydration matters more than people think. Persistent diarrhoea, meaning more than a couple of weeks, always warrants assessment rather than self-management.

The Bristol scale. The single most useful thing you can track, and the language your doctor already speaks.

Chapter Eleven · Reflux

Chapter Eleven

Reflux

Usually a mechanical problem with a valve, not a chemical problem with acid.

At the join between oesophagus and stomach there is a ring of muscle that should stay closed except when swallowing. Reflux happens when it relaxes at the wrong moment, or when pressure below it pushes contents up past it.

Note what that means: the problem is usually not that you are making too much acid. Your stomach is supposed to be acidic. The problem is that acid is arriving somewhere with no protection against it.

This matters because the instinctive response, suppressing acid, treats the symptom rather than the mechanism. Acid-suppressing medication is genuinely useful and sometimes necessary, particularly where there is inflammation to heal. But long-term use has consequences: reduced absorption of B12, iron, magnesium and calcium, and a stomach less able to sterilise what arrives. It is worth reviewing periodically rather than continuing by default.

Stopping abruptly after long use often causes rebound, which people understandably read as proof they needed it. Any reduction should be gradual and discussed.

What genuinely reduces reflux

1Smaller meals, eaten earlier. Volume raises pressure below the valve. Leaving three hours between the last meal and lying down is one of the most effective single changes.
2Raise the head of the bed by ten to twenty centimetres, under the legs rather than with extra pillows, which just bend you in the middle.
3Identify your own triggers. Alcohol, coffee, chocolate, mint, fat and very large meals all relax the valve, but people differ. Track rather than assume.
4Loosen what is around your waist. Tight waistbands genuinely increase abdominal pressure.
5Consider alginates. They form a raft on top of the stomach contents and work mechanically rather than by suppressing acid. Often underused.

Chapter Twelve

IBS

A real condition with a real mechanism. Not a diagnosis of exclusion, and not a euphemism for having nothing wrong with you.

41
Chapter Twelve · IBS

Irritable bowel syndrome affects roughly one in ten people. It is characterised by abdominal pain related to defaecation, together with a change in stool frequency or form, persisting over months.

For years it was described as a diagnosis made when everything else had been ruled out, which left people feeling they had been told nothing was wrong. That framing is outdated. IBS has identifiable mechanisms: visceral hypersensitivity, where normal gut signals are perceived as painful; disordered motility; altered gut-brain signalling; low-grade immune activation in some cases; and changes in the microbiome. Many people can trace onset to a gut infection, which has its own name, post-infectious IBS.

It is usually described by its predominant pattern: constipation-predominant, diarrhoea-predominant, or mixed. The pattern matters, because the treatments diverge.

What must be excluded first

Coeliac disease, by blood test, while still eating gluten. Inflammatory bowel disease, usually via faecal calprotectin. Anaemia. And in the appropriate age group or with red flag symptoms, bowel cancer.

If nobody has done this, it is reasonable to ask why.

What the evidence supports

1Soluble fibre, particularly psyllium. One of the better-evidenced dietary interventions. Insoluble fibre such as wheat bran frequently makes IBS worse, which is why generic “eat more fibre” advice sometimes backfires badly here.
2The low FODMAP diet, done properly. Genuinely effective for many, and genuinely risky when done wrong. It is a diagnostic tool: two to six weeks of restriction, then structured reintroduction to find your own thresholds. It was never designed to be permanent.
3Peppermint oil capsules. Reasonable evidence for pain and spasm, cheap, and low risk.
4Gut-directed hypnotherapy and CBT. Among the strongest evidence bases in the whole of IBS management, with effects comparable to dietary intervention and better durability. Consistently under-offered.
5Regular meals, sleep and movement. Unglamorous, and they do more than most supplements aimed at this condition.

On low FODMAP specifically

Please do it with a dietitian if you can. The restriction phase removes many of the exact foods that feed your microbiome, and staying on it long term reduces diversity. The reintroduction phase is the point of the whole exercise, and it is the phase people skip. A diet that helps you at the cost of permanently shrinking what you eat has not really helped you.

If stress is clearly a trigger

Then treating the stress is not an alternative to treating the gut; it is treating the gut. Visceral hypersensitivity is modulated by the nervous system. This is why the psychological therapies work as well as they do, and it is not a suggestion that your symptoms are imagined.

Chapter Thirteen · SIBO

Chapter Thirteen

SIBO

Bacteria in the right body, in the wrong room.

The small intestine is meant to be relatively sparsely populated. The colon is where the dense microbial community belongs. Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth is what happens when colonic-type bacteria establish themselves higher up.

The consequence is that fermentation begins in the wrong place. Instead of fibre being fermented in the colon, carbohydrate is fermented in the small intestine, immediately after eating, producing gas where there is far less room for it. This is why the classic presentation is bloating that begins soon after meals rather than hours later, often with a visibly distended abdomen, and often worse with exactly the healthy high-fibre foods someone has just started eating.

The usual underlying cause is a motility problem, specifically a failure of the housekeeping wave from Chapter Two. Anything that impairs it can predispose: previous gut infection, abdominal surgery and adhesions, some medications, diabetes, hypothyroidism, and structural issues. This is why SIBO recurs so often when it is treated without addressing why it happened.

Diagnosis is usually by breath test, measuring hydrogen and methane after a sugar challenge. The tests are imperfect and both over- and under-diagnose, so the result should be read alongside the clinical picture rather than as a verdict.

What treatment looks like

Antibiotics, most commonly rifaximin, which acts largely within the gut. Sometimes herbal antimicrobial protocols, which have some supporting evidence though less than the pharmaceutical route. Then, crucially, addressing the underlying motility issue, which may include prokinetic medication and leaving genuine gaps between meals so the housekeeping wave can run. Dietary restriction can reduce symptoms during treatment but does not resolve the overgrowth, and prolonged restriction makes the microbiome worse.

An important caution

SIBO has become a fashionable diagnosis, and a great deal is attributed to it that is really IBS, or carbohydrate malabsorption, or constipation. If you have been told you have SIBO on the basis of a symptom questionnaire alone, or you are on your fourth round of treatment with no investigation of why, it is reasonable to step back and ask whether the diagnosis is right.

Chapter Fourteen

Intolerance, Allergy, Coeliac

Three completely different things, routinely spoken about as if they were one.

45
Chapter Fourteen · Intolerance

Intolerance

a digestive limit

No immune involvement. Your body lacks the enzyme, or the capacity, to handle a certain amount of something. Lactose intolerance is the classic example. Symptoms are dose-dependent, which is the key feature: a splash of milk in tea may be fine when a glass is not. It is a threshold, not a prohibition.

Allergy

an immune reaction

IgE-mediated, rapid, and potentially dangerous. Hives, swelling, wheeze, vomiting, anaphylaxis. Usually within minutes. This is diagnosed by a specialist with skin prick or specific IgE testing, interpreted alongside history. Not something to investigate alone.

Coeliac disease

an autoimmune condition

Gluten triggers the immune system to damage the small intestine lining. It is not an intolerance and it is not a preference. It affects around one per cent of people, is substantially underdiagnosed, and requires strict lifelong gluten avoidance because the damage continues silently even without symptoms.

The single most important practical fact in this chapter

If you suspect coeliac disease, get tested before you remove gluten. The blood test looks for the immune response to gluten, so if you have already stopped eating it, the test can come back negative even if you have the condition. Countless people cut gluten, feel better, and are then left permanently unable to get a clear answer, which matters because coeliac disease has implications for bone density, nutrient absorption, pregnancy and family screening. Ask for tissue transglutaminase IgA together with total IgA, while still eating gluten normally.

Non-coeliac gluten sensitivity

A real reported phenomenon, and an area of genuine scientific uncertainty. Some research suggests that for a proportion of people the trigger may be the fermentable carbohydrates in wheat rather than gluten itself. If coeliac disease has been properly excluded and you feel better without wheat, that is legitimate information about your own body. It is still worth knowing which component is responsible, because the answer determines how much of the food world you actually need to avoid.

46
Chapter Fifteen · Testing

What to Test, and What to Skip

The gut is the most over-tested and under-investigated part of the body at the same time.

Worth doing, when indicated

Coeliac serology — tTG-IgA with total IgA, while still eating gluten.

Faecal calprotectin — distinguishes inflammatory bowel disease from IBS. Genuinely useful and often the missing test.

Full blood count and ferritin — anaemia and iron deficiency are red flags and also common consequences.

Breath testing — hydrogen and methane, for SIBO or carbohydrate malabsorption, read alongside symptoms.

B12, folate, vitamin D — where absorption is in question.

Thyroid function — because hypothyroidism causes constipation and is easily missed.

Not worth your money

IgG food sensitivity panels. These are the big one. IgG antibodies to food indicate exposure, not intolerance — they are arguably a marker of a normal immune response. Major allergy and immunology bodies advise against them. They routinely return long lists of “reactive” foods and are a leading cause of unnecessary restriction.

Routine commercial microbiome panels. Interesting, rarely actionable.

Hair mineral analysis and applied kinesiology for food reactions. No validity.

Repeat stool parasitology in the absence of relevant travel or symptoms.

A word on the phrase “all my tests came back normal”

This is not the same as nothing being wrong. IBS, visceral hypersensitivity, motility disorders and functional dyspepsia are all real conditions with normal blood work by definition. Normal tests are genuinely good news, because they exclude the dangerous things. They are not a verdict on whether your symptoms exist.

Chapter Sixteen

Fibre

If this book could only make one recommendation, it would be this one, and it is not close.

51
Chapter Sixteen · Fibre

Most adults need somewhere between twenty-five and thirty-five grams of fibre a day. Most adults eat around half that. This single gap explains more digestive misery than any food anyone has been told to avoid.

Fibre is the part of plant food you cannot digest. That is the whole point of it: because you cannot break it down, it arrives in the colon intact, where your bacteria can. What they make from it, chiefly butyrate, is the primary fuel for the cells lining your colon.

Read that again, because it is the central fact of this book. The cells of your gut wall are fed by what your bacteria produce from your fibre. A low-fibre diet does not merely make stools harder. It underfeeds the barrier itself.

How to actually get there

1Go slowly. Adding twenty grams overnight produces gas, cramping and a firm conviction that fibre is the enemy. Add roughly five grams a week and give your microbiome time to adjust.
2Increase fluid at the same time. Fibre works by holding water. Without more water, more fibre makes constipation worse, which is exactly why some people conclude it does not work for them.
3Lead with legumes. Beans, lentils and chickpeas are the highest-yield change available. A single tin of lentils carries more fibre than most people manage in a day.
4Leave the skins on. Potatoes, apples, cucumbers. Free fibre for doing less work.
5Add rather than replace. A tablespoon of seeds on what you already eat, a handful of frozen peas into whatever is in the pan. Additions survive; substitutions get abandoned.
52
Chapter Sixteen · Fibre

Who should be careful

This is where most gut advice fails people, so it belongs here in full rather than as a footnote. A minority will feel worse on a rapid increase in fibre, and they deserve to know why rather than concluding they have failed.

Approach fibre carefully if you have

Active SIBO, where fermentable fibre feeds bacteria that are in the wrong place.

Severe IBS, particularly diarrhoea-predominant, where insoluble fibre often aggravates. Soluble fibre such as psyllium is usually far better tolerated.

Inflammatory bowel disease during a flare, when a lower-residue approach is often advised temporarily.

Any degree of bowel narrowing or obstruction, where fibre is genuinely unsafe and this is a medical matter.

Gastroparesis or significantly delayed stomach emptying.

What to do instead

Start with soluble fibre only, in small amounts. Cook vegetables rather than eating them raw, which is easier on a sensitive gut. Consider whether the underlying condition needs treating first, because in SIBO and IBD the fibre question cannot be answered until it is. And get individual guidance rather than following general advice, including the advice in this chapter.

The general rule

If increasing fibre gradually, with adequate fluid, consistently makes you worse rather than better, that is not a failure of willpower. It is clinical information, and it means something else needs looking at first.

Chapter Seventeen

Thirty Plants and a Jar of Something Live

Two habits, both cheap, both better evidenced than anything in a bottle.

54
Chapter Seventeen · Diversity

Different microbes feed on different fibres. This is why variety beats quantity, and why eating the same three vegetables in large amounts is less useful than eating twelve in small ones.

The most cited target in microbiome research, and the easiest to hit once you know what counts.

What counts toward thirty

Vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds, herbs and spices. A teaspoon of mixed herbs is several. A handful of mixed seeds is several more. Different colours of the same vegetable count separately. Coffee, tea, olive oil and dark chocolate contribute plant compounds too.

Most people who count for the first time land between eight and fifteen, and reach thirty within a fortnight simply by paying attention.

Fermented foods

One to two small servings daily is the target that has held up best. Live yoghurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, miso, fermented vegetables. Look for live or active cultures on the label and low added sugar; pasteurised sauerkraut in a shelf-stable jar contains nothing living.

Start small. A tablespoon, not a bowl. Fermented foods are among the few interventions shown to increase microbial diversity in controlled trials.

Chapter Eighteen

How You Eat

The same meal, eaten differently, is digested differently. This chapter costs nothing.

56
Chapter Eighteen · How You Eat

Five habits, in order of how much they change

1Chew until it is properly broken down. The only mechanical step you control. It increases surface area for enzymes, mixes food with saliva, and signals the rest of the tract to prepare. Most people chew a handful of times and swallow. Aim to notice the texture change before it goes.
2Sit down, and put the screen away. Digestion is a parasympathetic process. Eating while working, driving or scrolling keeps you in the state where digestion is deprioritised, and it also makes you eat faster and swallow more air.
3Leave gaps between meals. Three to four hours, so the housekeeping wave can run. Constant grazing is one of the more plausible contributors to bloating in otherwise healthy people.
4Drink enough water across the day. Not litres at once, and not needed only with fibre. Hydration is what makes fibre work rather than seize up.
5Walk for ten minutes afterwards. Stimulates motility, improves blood sugar response, and helps reflux by keeping you upright. The best-evidenced small habit in the entire book.

The three-breath rule

Before your first mouthful, take three slow breaths with the out-breath longer than the in-breath. This is not a wellness flourish. A longer exhale activates the parasympathetic branch of the nervous system, which is the state in which saliva, acid and enzymes are actually secreted and in which motility is coordinated. It takes fifteen seconds and it is the highest ratio of benefit to effort available to you.

Chapter Nineteen · Stress

Chapter Nineteen

Stress, and the Nerve That Carries It

If your symptoms track your life rather than your meals, this is the chapter for you.

When the sympathetic nervous system is active, digestion is deprioritised in a coordinated way. Blood flow to the gut falls. Secretion of acid and enzymes reduces. Upper motility slows and lower motility speeds. And visceral sensitivity rises, so that ordinary internal sensations begin to register as discomfort or pain.

Every one of those is adaptive if you are in danger. None of them is helpful across a decade of low-grade pressure with no resolution.

This is why identical diets produce different outcomes in the same person at different times of life, and why so many people find their carefully engineered food protocol stops working during a hard year. The food did not change. The nervous system did.

The intervention is not to eliminate stress, which is not on offer. It is to build reliable moments of recovery, and to place at least one of them immediately before eating, when it does the most good.

What works, ordered by evidence

1Slow breathing with a long exhale. In for four, out for six or eight, for two minutes. Works within a minute, works on physiology directly, and does not require you to feel calm first.
2Gut-directed hypnotherapy. Genuinely one of the strongest evidence bases in functional gut disorders, with durable effects. Available as apps and structured programmes as well as in person.
3Cognitive behavioural therapy, particularly where there is anxiety about symptoms themselves, which is extremely common and creates its own loop.
4Regular sleep. Short sleep worsens visceral sensitivity and motility, and it is often the first thing to go in the periods when symptoms flare.
5Daily movement, not intense training. Walking is enough. Heavy exercise during a flare frequently makes things worse.

The loop worth naming

Symptoms cause anxiety about symptoms. Anxiety increases visceral sensitivity. Increased sensitivity produces more symptoms. Many people spend years attacking this loop from the food side only, when it has two doors and the other one is often easier to open.

This is not “it is all in your head”

Visceral hypersensitivity is measurable. Motility changes are measurable. These are physical events with a psychological trigger, which is an entirely different statement from saying you are imagining them. Anyone who has ever felt sick with dread already knows this is real.

Finding Your Triggers

How to do this without ending up with a diet of six foods and a fear of restaurants.

Chapter Twenty · Triggers

Elimination is a legitimate diagnostic tool and a terrible lifestyle. The difference is entirely in the method.

The safe method

1Keep a diary first, and change nothing. Two to four weeks recording what you ate, symptoms, timing, stools by Bristol type, sleep and stress. Most people find a pattern here without removing anything, and it is often not the pattern they expected.
2Remove one group only. Not four. One, chosen because the diary pointed at it, for two to six weeks. Removing several at once means that if you improve you will never know which mattered.
3Track through the removal. Same measures as before, so you are comparing like with like rather than relying on a general impression.
4Reintroduce deliberately. This is the part people skip and it is the whole point. Reintroduce in increasing amounts across a few days, then wait, then move to the next. You are looking for your threshold, not a yes or no.
5Put it back if it was innocent. Foods removed “just in case” tend to stay removed for years. Every unnecessary exclusion costs you microbial diversity and social ease, and buys nothing.

When to stop and get help

If you are anxious before eating. If you are avoiding restaurants, or other people’s houses, or travel. If your list of safe foods is getting shorter each month. If you feel guilt after eating. If you have removed several food groups without a professional involved. If eating has become a subject you think about constantly.

These are not signs of dedication. They are signs that the approach has begun to cost more than the symptoms did, and it is worth speaking to a dietitian or your doctor. A narrower diet is very often a less healthy gut, which means the strategy can end up working directly against the goal.

Chapter Twenty-One · Progress

The four things worth tracking

Track few things, consistently. A long list gets abandoned by week two, and the data you actually need is small.

Stool form

Bristol type, daily

The single most informative measure. It reflects transit speed directly, it is objective, it takes two seconds, and it is the language your doctor already uses.

Frequency

how often, and when

Anywhere from three times a day to three times a week is normal. What matters is that it is regular for you and comfortable. A change in your own pattern is more meaningful than the absolute number.

Bloating

timing and severity out of ten

Record when it starts relative to eating, not just that it happened. Immediately after a meal, hours later, or all evening regardless are three different problems.

Plants per week

the count

The one input worth measuring, because it is the one that moves everything else. Counting for a single week is usually enough to reveal the gap.

Give it three months before judging

Symptoms can improve in one to two weeks. The microbiome itself changes composition within days but takes weeks to months to shift meaningfully and stably. If you change something and reassess after ten days, you will conclude it failed. Almost everything in this book needs a season.

Your Four Weeks

Your Four Weeks

One change a week. Nothing removed. Everything additive.

The plan

1Week one: measure, change nothing. Record Bristol type daily, note bloating and timing, and count your plants for the week. That number is your baseline and you will want it later.
2Week two: add legumes and a walk. One serving of beans, lentils or chickpeas most days, and ten minutes of walking after your largest meal. Keep recording.
3Week three: add plants and something live. Push toward thirty different plants across the week, and add a small daily serving of a fermented food. Start with a tablespoon.
4Week four: change how you eat. Three slow breaths before the first bite. Sitting down, screen away, for at least one meal a day. Three to four hour gaps between meals.
5Then hold all four for eight more weeks before you judge any of it, and before you consider removing a single thing.

What to expect along the way

Days two to five of week two may be gassier. That is fermentation adjusting, not a sign that legumes are your enemy. Rinse tinned pulses well, start with a few tablespoons rather than a full portion, and it usually settles inside a week. If it does not settle at all after a fortnight, that is worth noting rather than pushing through.

The only rule

Nothing is removed in these four weeks. Every step adds something. If at the end of twelve weeks a food genuinely needs testing, you will do it properly, with a diary behind you and a reintroduction planned. That is a very different act from cutting things out in hope.

Closing

What to Take With You

Your gut is doing four jobs, not one: breaking food down, taking it up, moving it along, and deciding what crosses into you. Most confusion in this subject comes from treating a problem in one of those as though it were a problem in another.

The microbiome is an ecosystem, and what predicts health is diversity rather than the presence of any particular organism. You feed it with variety, and it feeds your gut wall back in the form of short-chain fats. That exchange is the centre of this entire book.

The connection to your mind is real and runs in both directions, but the strongest arrow points the way that is least advertised: your nervous system is one of the most powerful influences on your digestion. Which means calm is not a luxury item here. It is treatment.

Fibre and plant variety do more than everything sold in bottles combined. How you eat matters nearly as much as what. And removal is a diagnostic tool with a defined end, never a way of life.

Finally: track something objective, give it three months, and know the red flags well enough that you would recognise them. Everything else is patience.

Your next step

Turn the page. Record one week without changing anything at all. Bristol type, bloating and timing, and a count of your plants. That single week will tell you more about your gut than any test you can buy.

Feed it well, move it along, and stop frightening it. Most of the rest looks after itself.
