Introduction

How to Use This Book

There are three ways into these pages, and none of them is wrong.

If you want to understand, begin at Part One. It explains how the endocrine system actually works: not as a list of hormones, but as a conversation between organs that never stops and never happens in isolation.

If you want to know what is normal for you now, go to Part Two. It follows a woman’s hormonal life in order, from the ovaries that formed while you were still a fetus to the decades after your last period. Each stage has its own physiology and its own reasonable expectations.

And if you came here because something specific is wrong, go straight to Part Three. The Symptom Finder starts with what you are feeling and works backwards toward what might be driving it.

A necessary note

This book is designed to make you a better-informed participant in your own care. It is not a diagnostic tool, and it cannot replace a clinician who can examine you, test you, and know your history.

Where something warrants medical attention, the text says so plainly. Please take those moments seriously.

The promise

Nothing here is designed to make you afraid of your own body.

The method

Mechanism first. If you understand why, the what becomes obvious.

The honesty

Where evidence is strong, we say so. Where it is thin, we say that too.

Chapter One

The Body’s Inner Conversation

Hormones are messages. What matters is who is speaking, who is listening, and what happens when the line goes quiet.

10
Chapter One · The Inner Conversation

A hormone is simply a message sent through the bloodstream. A gland releases it, it travels, and somewhere else in the body a cell with the matching receptor receives it and changes what it is doing. That is the whole idea.

What makes the endocrine system difficult is not the messages. It is that everything is connected to everything. No hormone acts alone. Each one is produced in response to others, cleared by organs that have their own demands, and heard by receptors whose sensitivity changes with sleep, stress, nutrition, inflammation and age.

This is why a woman with irregular cycles may be told her ovaries are the problem when the actual driver is her thyroid, her insulin, her stress physiology, or simply that she is not eating enough to convince her brain it is safe to reproduce.

Symptoms rarely have a single cause. They have a network of contributing factors, and the useful question is almost never which hormone is broken but where in the conversation did the signal get lost.

The glands, and what they answer for

Hypothalamus, the sensor. Monitors stress, energy, light and body weight, then decides whether reproduction is affordable right now.
Pituitary, the relay. Converts the hypothalamus’s instructions into FSH, LH, TSH, ACTH and prolactin.
Thyroid, the pace-setter. Governs the metabolic rate of every cell you own.
Adrenals, the responders. Cortisol, adrenaline, DHEA and a meaningful share of your androgens.
Pancreas, the fuel manager. Insulin and glucagon, and far more influence over your cycle than most people are told.
Ovaries, the cyclical organ. Oestrogen, progesterone, testosterone and AMH, on a rhythm that repeats for roughly four decades.
11
Chapter One · The Inner Conversation

Loops, not ladders

The single most useful thing to understand about hormones is that they run on feedback loops, the way a thermostat does. The brain sends a signal, the gland responds, and the response travels back to the brain to adjust the next signal. Nothing is one-directional.

The hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis. The dotted lines are the whole story: the ovary answers back, and the brain listens.

This is why hormone results are so easily misread. A low number can mean the gland is struggling, or it can mean the brain has quite sensibly turned the signal down. The number alone does not tell you which.

It is also why the timing of a blood test matters enormously in women. Measure progesterone in the first week of a cycle and it will be low, because it is supposed to be. The result is not reassuring or alarming. It is meaningless.

Three axes, one system

The same architecture repeats throughout the body. The HPO axis governs reproduction. The HPA axis governs stress. The HPT axis governs the thyroid. They share the same hypothalamus and the same pituitary, which is precisely why stress can silence a cycle and a thyroid problem can cause infertility.

Chapter Two · The Cast

Chapter Two

The Cast

Sex hormones get the attention. These are the ones quietly deciding what the sex hormones are allowed to do.

Oestrogen

the builder

Chiefly oestradiol during the reproductive years. Thickens the uterine lining, maintains bone density, supports skin collagen, vaginal tissue, cardiovascular function, mood and memory. Rises through the first half of the cycle. Its decline is what makes menopause a whole-body event rather than simply the end of periods.

Progesterone

the steadier

Made by the corpus luteum only after ovulation. Stabilises the uterine lining, raises resting body temperature, and calms the nervous system through its metabolite allopregnanolone. No ovulation means no meaningful progesterone, which is why anovulatory cycles so often feel anxious and sleepless.

Testosterone

not a male hormone

Women produce it in the ovaries and adrenals, in smaller amounts than men but to real effect: libido, muscle, bone, motivation, clarity. Too little is under-recognised. Too much, usually driven by insulin, produces the acne, scalp hair thinning and unwanted hair growth seen in PCOS.

FSH & LH

the instructions

Released by the pituitary. FSH recruits a cohort of follicles each month and coaxes one to maturity. LH triggers ovulation in a sharp surge and then sustains the corpus luteum. Their pattern, more than their absolute value, is what a clinician is reading.

13
Chapter Two · The Cast

Cortisol

the override

Adrenal, and rhythmic: highest shortly after waking, lowest around midnight. Essential in short bursts. When it stays elevated it suppresses the hypothalamus, blunts ovulation, disrupts sleep, raises inflammation and worsens insulin resistance. Chapter Three is devoted to it.

Insulin

the fuel manager

Rarely discussed as a reproductive hormone, and it should be. Insulin drives ovarian androgen production, lowers the binding protein that keeps testosterone in check, and influences ovulation directly. Metabolic health and hormonal health are not separate subjects.

Thyroid hormones

the tempo

T4 is produced in quantity and converted to the active T3 in the tissues. They set the metabolic speed of every cell. Under-function causes heavy or irregular bleeding, fatigue, cold intolerance, anovulation and raised prolactin. Over-function shortens or stops cycles.

Prolactin

the interrupter

Enables lactation and, when elevated, suppresses the reproductive axis. This is deliberate during breastfeeding. Outside it, high prolactin causes irregular or absent periods and needs investigation. It is also raised by stress, sleep loss and some medications.

DHEA

the precursor

An adrenal androgen and a raw material the body converts into other hormones. Peaks in the twenties and declines steadily. Frequently sold as a supplement; it is a genuine hormone and deserves testing before use, not enthusiasm.

Melatonin, leptin & ghrelin

the context

Melatonin carries darkness as information and has receptors in the ovary. Leptin, made by fat tissue, tells the hypothalamus whether there is enough stored energy to sustain a pregnancy. Ghrelin signals hunger. Together they explain why light, sleep and under-eating are hormonal issues.

The thread running through all of this

Almost every hormone above is affected by four inputs: how you sleep, how you eat, how you move, and how much unresolved stress you carry. This is not a coincidence, and it is not a platitude. It is the reason the last chapter of this book is short.

Chapter Three

Cortisol, the Survival Override

Your body will not invest in making a baby during what it believes to be a famine, a war, or a crisis that never ends.

16
Chapter Three · Cortisol

Cortisol is not the villain. It is the hormone that gets you out of bed, mobilises fuel, sharpens attention and holds inflammation in check. A healthy cortisol curve is steep in the morning and low at night, and you want that curve.

The problem is not cortisol. It is cortisol that never comes down.

The hypothalamus is, above everything else, a risk assessor. It is continuously sampling the environment: is there enough food, enough rest, enough safety? Reproduction is metabolically the most expensive thing a female body can undertake. When the answer to those questions is no, the hypothalamus reduces the pulsing of GnRH, the signal that starts the entire reproductive cascade.

Ovulation is delayed, weakened, or does not happen at all. And because progesterone is made only after ovulation, a stressed cycle is very often a low-progesterone cycle: shorter luteal phase, worse sleep, more anxiety, heavier or earlier bleeding.

A myth worth retiring

You may have read that cortisol “steals” pregnenolone from progesterone production, leaving nothing for your sex hormones. It is a memorable image and it is not how steroid synthesis works: these pathways occur in different tissues and are not competing for one shared pool.

The real mechanism is simpler and better evidenced. Stress signalling suppresses the brain’s reproductive output directly. The outcome is similar. The explanation matters, because it tells you where to intervene.

Chronic stress does not damage the ovary. It quiets the instruction that reaches it.

17
Chapter Three · Cortisol

What the body counts as stress

This is where most people underestimate their own physiology. The hypothalamus does not distinguish between kinds of threat. It sums them.

Psychological pressure counts. So does under-eating, whether deliberate or accidental. So does over-training without adequate fuel. So does chronic sleep deprivation, untreated pain, illness, inflammation, and the low-grade vigilance of a life with no margin in it.

A woman can be eating what looks like a reasonable amount, training five days a week, sleeping six hours and managing a demanding job, and her hypothalamus reads the total as scarcity. The cycle lengthens. Then it disappears. This is called functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea, and it is common, reversible, and frequently missed.

Critically, it is not solved by a supplement. It is solved by removing load: more food, more sleep, less training, or less pressure. Usually more than one.

What genuinely lowers the load

1Protect sleep before anything else. Cortisol regulation is largely nocturnal. A consistent wake time is the single highest-leverage intervention in this chapter.
2Eat enough, and eat early. Skipping breakfast and under-fuelling reads as scarcity. Eating within a couple of hours of waking, with protein, steadies both cortisol and blood sugar.
3Lengthen the exhale. Breathing in for four and out for six to eight for two minutes activates the parasympathetic response directly. It works even when your mind refuses to cooperate.
4Match training to recovery. If your cycle has lengthened or stopped, high-intensity work is part of the picture, not the solution to it. Walking, strength work and rest days are not a downgrade.
5Get morning light and evening dark. The cortisol curve is anchored by the light-dark cycle more than by anything else you do.

When to seek help rather than manage it alone

Three or more months without a period, when you are not pregnant or breastfeeding, always warrants investigation. So does a cycle that has become consistently shorter than 21 days or longer than 35, or a luteal phase you can track as shorter than about ten days. These are not things to wait out.

Chapter Four

Insulin, the Hormone Nobody Mentions

It is filed under blood sugar. It belongs in any honest conversation about ovulation, androgens and fertility.

20
Chapter Four · Insulin

Insulin’s job is to move glucose out of the bloodstream and into cells. When cells stop responding well to that instruction, the pancreas compensates by producing more. Blood sugar can look entirely normal for years while insulin runs high underneath it.

That elevated insulin is not metabolically neutral. In the ovary, it acts on the theca cells to increase androgen production. In the liver, it suppresses sex hormone binding globulin, the protein that keeps testosterone bound and inactive. Less binding protein means more free testosterone circulating, from the same total amount.

The visible result is acne along the jaw, hair thinning at the crown, unwanted hair growth, and cycles that stretch out and become unpredictable as ovulation falters. The invisible result is a loop that reinforces itself.

Why this matters even if your glucose is normal

A standard fasting glucose test is the last thing to change. By the time it is abnormal, insulin has usually been elevated for years. If insulin resistance is suspected, fasting insulin, HbA1c and the ratio between glucose and insulin give a far earlier picture than glucose alone. Ask for them by name.

21
Chapter Four · Insulin

Working with insulin, gently

Nothing in this section requires a restrictive diet, and this book will not give you one. Insulin sensitivity responds to how you eat and move at least as much as to what you remove.

Five things that reliably help

1Walk after meals. Ten minutes of gentle movement after eating meaningfully lowers the glucose and insulin response. It is the best-evidenced small habit in this chapter.
2Anchor meals with protein and fibre. Not to replace carbohydrate, but to change the shape of the curve. Eating vegetables and protein before starch lowers the same meal’s response.
3Build muscle. Muscle is the largest site of glucose disposal in the body. Resistance training two or three times a week improves insulin sensitivity independently of weight change.
4Protect sleep. A few nights of short sleep measurably worsens insulin sensitivity in healthy people. This is not a small effect.
5Do not skip meals to compensate. Long gaps followed by large meals produce sharper spikes and reinforce the pattern. Regular, adequate meals work better than restriction.

A word on how this is often discussed

Insulin resistance is frequently framed as a personal failing, and it is not. Genetics, ethnicity, sleep, stress, medication, PCOS and life stage all contribute. Perimenopause alone reduces insulin sensitivity. The response is practical, not moral.

Where this is going

Insulin is the thread connecting this chapter to PCOS on page 42, to inflammation, and to the metabolic shifts of perimenopause on page 50. If you read only two chapters in Part One, read this one and the next.

Chapter Five

The Estrobolome

Your body does not only make oestrogen. It has to get rid of it too, and that job belongs largely to your gut.

23
Chapter Five · The Estrobolome

Hormonal balance is not only a question of production. It is equally a question of clearance, and clearance is where a surprising amount goes wrong.

Oestrogen made in the ovary travels to the liver, where it is chemically packaged so it can be excreted safely. That package leaves the liver in bile and enters the intestine, and at that point it is meant to leave the body.

The estrobolome is the collection of gut bacteria that carry an enzyme called beta-glucuronidase. This enzyme can unwrap the package, freeing oestrogen back into its active form so that it is reabsorbed into circulation instead of eliminated.

A balanced microbiome keeps this recycling modest and useful. A disrupted one, from a low-fibre diet, repeated antibiotics, chronic inflammation or constipation, can push it too far. The result is a higher circulating oestrogen load without the ovaries producing a single extra molecule.

Why this shows up as symptoms

Oestrogen dominance is best understood as a ratio problem rather than a raw number. Symptoms such as breast tenderness, heavy or clotted bleeding, worsening PMS, bloating and cycle-linked headaches often reflect oestrogen that is high relative to progesterone.

That ratio can shift because progesterone is low, because oestrogen is high, or because oestrogen is not leaving efficiently. All three routes lead to a similar week.

Production, packaging, elimination, and the loop that returns it to circulation.

24
Chapter Five · The Estrobolome

Supporting the exit route

What the evidence supports

1Fibre, in variety and quantity. Fibre binds packaged oestrogen in the gut and carries it out. It also feeds the bacteria that keep the microbiome balanced. Most women eat roughly half of what is recommended.
2Cruciferous vegetables. Broccoli, cabbage, rocket, cauliflower and their relatives supply compounds that support the liver’s oestrogen-processing pathways. Regular modest amounts, not heroic ones.
3A daily bowel movement. Unglamorous and genuinely relevant. Constipation increases the time available for reabsorption. Water, fibre, magnesium and movement all help.
4Plant diversity over supplement stacks. Around thirty different plant foods a week is a well-supported target for microbial diversity, and herbs, spices, nuts and seeds all count toward it.
5Moderate alcohol. Alcohol both raises circulating oestrogen and competes for the liver capacity needed to clear it.

What this chapter does not claim

You will see the estrobolome used to sell detox protocols, expensive microbiome panels and long supplement lists. The underlying biology is real and well documented. The commercial applications built on top of it are mostly not. Fibre, plants, regular bowel habits and moderate alcohol are the interventions with actual support behind them, and they are cheap.

The link forward

Gut health, insulin and stress are not three separate chapters that happen to sit near each other. They are three doors into the same room. This is why women so often find that the thing which finally settles their cycle had nothing to do with their ovaries.

Chapter Six

The Thyroid and the Quieter Voices

A butterfly-shaped gland at the base of the throat sets the speed of every cell you own, including the ones that run your cycle.

26
Chapter Six · Thyroid

The thyroid is the most commonly missed contributor to hormonal symptoms in women, and the most easily tested.

The pituitary releases TSH, which instructs the thyroid to produce T4. T4 is then converted in the tissues to T3, the active form that actually enters cells and sets their metabolic pace. Selenium, zinc, iron and adequate calories are all required for that conversion, which is one reason under-eating and thyroid symptoms so often travel together.

When thyroid output is low, cycles tend to become heavier, longer or irregular. Ovulation may stop. Prolactin can rise, which suppresses the reproductive axis further. Fatigue, cold hands, constipation, dry skin, hair thinning and low mood follow. Miscarriage risk rises, which is why thyroid function is checked in fertility work-ups.

When it is high, cycles shorten or disappear, alongside anxiety, palpitations, heat intolerance and weight loss.

What to ask for

A TSH alone is a screening test, not a full picture. If symptoms are present, ask for TSH, free T4, free T3 and thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies. Antibodies can be positive years before the numbers drift, and autoimmune thyroid disease is substantially more common in women. It also frequently appears or worsens in the year after giving birth.

27
Chapter Six · Thyroid

The quieter voices

Prolactin

when it is not breastfeeding

Raised prolactin suppresses GnRH and therefore ovulation. Causes include an underactive thyroid, pituitary adenomas (usually benign and treatable), several common medications including some antidepressants and anti-nausea drugs, and significant stress. It is measured in the morning, ideally rested, and not after exercise, sex or a breast examination, all of which raise it temporarily.

Melatonin

darkness as information

Released by the pineal gland in response to darkness. It sets circadian timing, and it has receptors in the ovary and on the follicle itself, where it appears to act as an antioxidant during maturation. Light at night suppresses it. This is one of several reasons shift work is associated with cycle disruption.

Leptin

the permission signal

Produced by fat tissue in proportion to how much of it you have, and read by the hypothalamus as a report on stored energy. Below a certain threshold the reproductive axis is switched off. Leptin is the mechanism connecting very low body fat, or rapid weight loss, to a cycle that quietly stops.

DHEA and androgens

the adrenal contribution

The adrenal glands make DHEA and a meaningful proportion of a woman’s androgens. DHEA peaks in the twenties and declines with age. When androgen symptoms appear, the source may be ovarian, adrenal, or driven by insulin, and the distinction changes the treatment entirely. It is worth establishing before assuming.

How to hold all of this

You do not need to memorise a cast list. You need one habit: when a hormonal symptom appears, ask what else could be driving it before accepting the most obvious answer. Thyroid, prolactin, insulin, stress and nutrition are the five that most often turn out to be the real story.

Chapter Seven

Before You Were Born

Every egg you will ever have was already made while your mother was pregnant with you.

32
Chapter Seven · Before You Were Born

It is worth sitting with that sentence for a moment, because almost nothing else in the body works this way.

At around five weeks of gestation, the embryo forms a gonadal ridge that is identical in every fetus. It is not yet ovary or testis. In the absence of the signals produced by a Y chromosome, that tissue follows the ovarian pathway, and the germ cells within it begin multiplying at extraordinary speed.

By roughly twenty weeks of gestation, that fetus, still in the womb, carries somewhere between six and seven million eggs. It is the highest number she will ever have. From that peak, the count only falls.

By birth, around one to two million remain. By puberty, perhaps three hundred thousand. Across an entire reproductive life, only around four hundred will ever be ovulated. The rest are lost steadily, silently, through a programmed process that continues regardless of contraception, pregnancy or lifestyle.

Why it matters practically

This is the biology underneath the phrase “ovarian reserve”. It cannot be increased. No supplement, diet or protocol makes new eggs.

What can be influenced is the environment those eggs mature in during the roughly three months before ovulation, and the age at which you are working with them. Both matter. Neither changes the total.

The only curve in the body that peaks before birth.

Chapter Eight

Becoming

Puberty is not one event. It is a sequence, and it happens in a reliable order.

34
Chapter Eight · Becoming

The first change is invisible. Around ages six to eight, the adrenal glands begin producing more DHEA in a phase called adrenarche. This is what causes the first body odour, oilier skin and early pubic hair, and it happens before the ovaries have woken up at all.

The reproductive axis itself reactivates later. It was briefly active in infancy, then dormant through childhood. When the hypothalamus resumes pulsing GnRH, usually at night at first, true puberty begins.

Thelarche, breast budding, is typically the first visible sign, most often between eight and thirteen. Then the growth spurt. Then menarche, the first period, on average around twelve to thirteen and usually about two years after breast development starts.

What is rarely explained is what follows. For the first year or two after menarche, many cycles are anovulatory: bleeding occurs without ovulation. Cycles are often irregular, sometimes very. This is normal maturation of the axis, not a disorder, and it is why a PCOS diagnosis should not rest on ultrasound findings within about eight years of menarche.

Skeletal maturity also matters here. The years around puberty are when peak bone mass is largely laid down, and oestrogen is the hormone that does it. A cycle lost to under-eating or over-training in adolescence has consequences for bone that are difficult to recover later.

One life, drawn as hormones. The reproductive years are the loud middle section, not the whole story.

What a young woman deserves to be told

That irregularity in the first two years is expected. That periods should not be so painful that school is missed, and that pain of that severity is a reason to be assessed rather than endured. That absent periods for months at a time are a signal, not a convenience. And that the cycle is a vital sign: something that tells you how the whole system is doing, not merely a fertility function.

Chapter Nine

The Cycle, Properly Explained

Not a monthly inconvenience. A carefully sequenced conversation between brain, ovary and uterus, repeated for four decades.

36
Chapter Nine · The Cycle

The cycle is counted from the first day of real bleeding, which is day one. Everything else is measured from there.

Cycle length varies between women and between months. The luteal phase is the part that stays relatively fixed.

The follicular phase · days 1 to roughly 13

FSH from the pituitary recruits a cohort of follicles, and one becomes dominant. As it grows it produces rising oestrogen, which rebuilds the uterine lining shed during the period. Energy, mood, skin and libido typically lift through this phase. Cervical mucus becomes wetter and more slippery as oestrogen climbs. This is the phase whose length varies; a long cycle is usually a long follicular phase.

Ovulation · around the middle

Once oestrogen passes a threshold and holds it, the feedback flips from negative to positive and the pituitary releases a sharp LH surge. The egg is released roughly a day after the surge begins. Some women feel this as a one-sided ache. Fertile-quality mucus, clear and stretchy, peaks in the days just before.

37
Chapter Nine · The Cycle

Oestrogen rises twice; progesterone only once, and only after ovulation.

The luteal phase

The follicle that released the egg does not disappear. It reorganises into a temporary gland called the corpus luteum, and its job is to produce progesterone.

Progesterone stabilises the uterine lining, raises resting body temperature by a small but measurable amount, and calms the nervous system through its metabolite allopregnanolone, which acts on the same receptors as anti-anxiety medication.

The corpus luteum has a fixed lifespan of roughly twelve to fourteen days unless a pregnancy signals it to continue. If no pregnancy occurs, it breaks down, progesterone and oestrogen fall sharply, and that withdrawal is what triggers the lining to shed. A new cycle begins.

This is the single most useful fact in the chapter: progesterone exists only because ovulation happened. A cycle without ovulation still bleeds, but it is a different physiological event, and it feels different.

Why the second half feels harder

PMS symptoms cluster in the late luteal phase because that is when both hormones are falling at once. The nervous system loses progesterone’s calming influence at the same moment oestrogen drops.

This is normal in mild form. Severe, disabling mood change in the week before bleeding, particularly if it lifts within a day or two of the period starting, may be PMDD, which is a recognised condition with real treatments. It is not something to tolerate quietly.

38
Chapter Nine · The Cycle

One follicle, five acts

A follicle spends roughly three months maturing before the cycle in which it is finally chosen.

The three-month window

The follicle ovulated this month began its journey around ninety days ago. This is genuinely useful: it means changes to sleep, nutrition, stress and alcohol act on eggs that will be released a season from now, not tomorrow. It also explains why any intervention needs about three cycles before it is fair to judge it.

What tracking actually tells you

Cervical mucus and basal body temperature together are more informative than either alone. Mucus predicts ovulation as it approaches. Temperature confirms it happened, but only after the fact. Ovulation predictor kits detect the LH surge, which is helpful, though a surge does not always guarantee an egg was released.

Chapter Nine · A Healthy Cycle

What a Healthy Cycle Looks Like

Use this as a reference point, not a standard to fail against.

Generally reassuring

Cycle length between 21 and 35 days, and reasonably consistent for you.
Bleeding for two to seven days.
Blood loss that does not soak through protection hourly or require you to change plans.
A luteal phase of at least ten days.
Cramping that responds to ordinary measures and does not stop your day.
Some premenstrual shift in mood or energy that resolves once bleeding begins.
Fertile-quality mucus for a few days mid-cycle.

Worth investigating

Cycles consistently shorter than 21 or longer than 35 days.
No period for three months or more outside pregnancy or breastfeeding.
Flooding, large clots, or bleeding that limits your life.
Bleeding between periods, or after sex.
Pain that needs you to miss work or school, or that is not helped by standard pain relief.
A luteal phase reliably shorter than ten days.
New, sudden change in a pattern that was previously stable.

A biphasic temperature pattern: the shift upward confirms that ovulation occurred.

The most important reframe in this book

Your cycle is a vital sign. Not a nuisance, not merely a fertility function, but a monthly report on whether your brain currently believes your body is well-fed, well-rested and safe. When it changes, it is telling you something about the whole system. That is worth reading rather than suppressing.

Chapter Ten

PCOS

The most common endocrine condition in women of reproductive age, and one of the most misunderstood.

42
Chapter Ten · PCOS

The name is unhelpful. Polycystic ovary syndrome does not involve cysts, and a woman can have it with entirely normal-looking ovaries. What the ultrasound shows is a larger than usual number of small, immature follicles that have stalled rather than maturing to ovulation.

Diagnosis rests on meeting two of three criteria, once other causes have been excluded: irregular or absent ovulation; clinical or laboratory evidence of raised androgens; and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound. In adults, an AMH level can now substitute for the scan. Within about eight years of the first period, ultrasound should not be used at all, because that appearance is common and normal in young women.

PCOS is not one thing. Insulin resistance is present in a large proportion of women with it, across all body sizes, and drives the androgen picture. In others, the dominant driver is adrenal, inflammatory, or a rebound after stopping hormonal contraception. The treatment that helps depends entirely on which pattern you have.

What it is not

It is not caused by being overweight, and it is not cured by weight loss, though insulin sensitivity often improves symptoms considerably.

It is not a barrier to pregnancy for most women, though it may take longer or need support.

And it is not resolved by the contraceptive pill. The pill manages symptoms well, and that is a legitimate choice, but it suppresses rather than restores ovulation.

The long view

PCOS carries increased long-term risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and, where cycles are very infrequent, endometrial changes from a lining that is rarely shed. This is precisely why it deserves attention beyond fertility, and why metabolic care in your thirties matters as much as cycle regulation in your twenties.

43
Chapter Ten · PCOS

Working with it

Where the evidence is strongest

1Improve insulin sensitivity first. Resistance training, daily walking, post-meal movement, adequate protein and fibre, and protected sleep. This addresses the driver rather than the downstream symptom.
2Do not under-eat. Aggressive restriction raises cortisol and often worsens cycles. Regular adequate meals outperform restriction here consistently.
3Ask about metformin and inositol. Both target insulin. Myo-inositol in particular has reasonable evidence for ovulation and cycle regularity, and a good safety profile. Discuss with your clinician rather than self-prescribing.
4Track ovulation rather than assuming it. Many women with PCOS do ovulate, just less predictably. Knowing whether and when changes everything about planning.
5Screen metabolically, on a schedule. HbA1c or an oral glucose tolerance test, lipids and blood pressure, repeated periodically. This is the part most often neglected.

The androgen symptoms

Acne, hair thinning at the crown and unwanted facial or body hair are the symptoms women most want addressed, and they are the slowest to respond, because hair follicles work on long cycles. Six months is a fair trial of anything. Topical and prescription options exist and are worth discussing; addressing insulin alongside them makes them work better.

What to say at the appointment

“I would like my insulin and HbA1c checked, not just glucose. I would like to know whether I am ovulating. And I would like to understand which driver is most likely in my case, rather than starting with symptom suppression.” That is a reasonable and well-informed request.

Chapter Eleven

Endometriosis

Tissue similar to the uterine lining, growing where it should not, responding to every hormonal signal it receives.

45
Chapter Eleven · Endometriosis

Endometriosis affects roughly one in ten women of reproductive age. The average delay between first symptoms and diagnosis is still measured in years, and the most common reason is that severe period pain has been normalised by everyone involved, including the person experiencing it.

The deposits respond to hormonal signalling much as the uterine lining does: they thicken and bleed cyclically. But they have nowhere to drain. The result is inflammation, scarring and adhesions that can bind organs together.

Symptoms include severe period pain, pain during or after sex, pain with bowel movements or urination particularly around menstruation, chronic pelvic pain, profound fatigue, and difficulty conceiving. Bowel symptoms are common enough that many women are treated for IBS for years first.

Crucially, the extent of disease does not predict the amount of pain. Minimal disease can be agonising; extensive disease can be nearly silent. Anyone who dismisses your pain because a scan looked unremarkable has misunderstood the condition.

Getting to a diagnosis

Laparoscopy remains the definitive test, but current guidance no longer requires surgery before treatment begins: diagnosis can be made on symptoms and imaging, and management started on that basis. A normal ultrasound does not exclude endometriosis. If you are being told to wait until it is bad enough to operate, it is reasonable to ask for referral to a specialist centre.

46
Chapter Eleven · Endometriosis

Living alongside it

Management usually combines several approaches, because no single one works for everyone. Hormonal treatments aim to reduce or stop cyclical bleeding, which reduces the stimulus to the deposits. Excision surgery by an experienced specialist can be highly effective; ablation is less so for deep disease.

Pain itself needs treating as its own problem. Long-standing pelvic pain changes how the nervous system processes signals, a process called central sensitisation, and at that point pain can persist even after disease is removed. Pelvic physiotherapy and pain-focused care are not consolation prizes; for many women they are the intervention that finally helps.

Anti-inflammatory nutrition, movement within tolerance, heat, sleep and stress work all have a supporting role. They will not remove the disease, and any source claiming a diet can cure endometriosis is not being honest with you. They can meaningfully change how much of your life it takes.

Fertility is affected for some women and not others. If you know you have endometriosis and hope to conceive, an earlier conversation is better than a later one, and that is worth raising even if it feels premature.

If you are trying to be believed

Bring a written record: dates, pain scores out of ten, what you have taken, what it stopped you doing. Name the impact rather than the sensation, because “I have missed four days of work this quarter” is heard differently from “my periods are painful”. Ask for the reason if you are declined, and ask for it to be recorded. You are allowed to request a second opinion.

A sentence worth carrying

Pain that stops your life is not a low pain threshold. It is information, and you are entitled to have it taken seriously.

Chapter Twelve

Fertility and the Year Before

Preconception is not a test to pass. It is a window in which small things compound unusually quickly.

48
Chapter Twelve · Fertility

The fertile window is shorter and earlier than most people assume. Sperm survive up to about five days in fertile cervical mucus; the egg survives roughly twenty-four hours. That gives a window of about six days, ending on the day of ovulation, with the highest chance in the two or three days before it.

This is why cervical mucus is more useful than temperature when trying to conceive. Temperature confirms ovulation after the window has closed. Mucus opens it.

Because eggs mature over roughly three months, the preconception period worth attending to is about ninety days, not a fortnight. And fertility is not a woman’s subject: sperm also take around three months to develop, and male factors contribute to roughly half of couples’ difficulties. Both partners are worth assessing from the start.

When to seek help

Standard guidance is to seek assessment after twelve months of trying if you are under 35, and after six months if you are 35 or older.

Sooner if cycles are irregular or absent, if you have known endometriosis or PCOS, if there is a history of pelvic infection or surgery, or if you have had recurrent miscarriage.

The preconception essentials

1Folate before conception, not after. Start at least a month before trying. The neural tube closes before most women know they are pregnant. A higher dose is advised in some circumstances, so ask.
2Check thyroid, iron and vitamin D. All three affect conception and early pregnancy, all three are commonly low, and all three are straightforward to correct.
3Confirm you are ovulating. Mucus tracking, an LH kit, or a progesterone test about seven days before your period is due. Regular bleeding alone does not prove it.
4Address insulin and thyroid before fertility treatment, not during. Both meaningfully affect outcomes and both take some months to settle.
5Stop smoking, moderate alcohol, and review medications with a clinician. Some common prescriptions need changing before conception rather than at the point of a positive test.

On the emotional weight of this chapter

Preconception advice can turn quickly into the sense that every choice is a test you might be failing. It is not. Most pregnancies happen in imperfect circumstances. These measures shift probability; they do not confer or withhold permission, and nothing here is a verdict on whether you deserve a pregnancy.

Chapter Thirteen

Pregnancy and the Fourth Trimester

The largest hormonal shift a human body undergoes, followed by the fastest withdrawal.

50
Chapter Thirteen · Pregnancy and After

If conception occurs, the developing embryo produces hCG, which signals the corpus luteum to keep making progesterone rather than breaking down. Around weeks eight to ten the placenta takes over that job, and hormone production shifts to an entirely new scale.

By late pregnancy, oestrogen and progesterone are many times higher than at any point in a normal cycle. Thyroid demand rises by roughly a third, which is why women with existing thyroid conditions need their dose reviewed early and why new thyroid problems can surface. Iron requirements rise substantially. Insulin resistance increases deliberately, to keep glucose available to the baby, which is the physiology gestational diabetes screening is looking at.

Then the placenta is delivered. Within a few days, oestrogen and progesterone fall from their highest ever levels to some of the lowest. There is no other moment in human physiology quite like it. Prolactin rises to support milk production and suppresses the reproductive axis, which is why cycles often pause during exclusive breastfeeding.

This is the hormonal context of the early postpartum weeks. It does not explain everything about that period, and it should never be used to dismiss what a woman is feeling, but it is real and it is worth knowing.

Three things that are missed too often

Postpartum thyroiditis affects around one in twenty women, typically appearing between three and twelve months after birth. It often begins with an overactive phase and moves to an underactive one, and it is regularly mistaken for ordinary new-parent exhaustion. It is a simple blood test.

Iron is frequently depleted by birth and rarely rechecked. Low ferritin causes fatigue, hair shedding and low mood, all of which get attributed to the baby.

Postnatal depression and anxiety are common, treatable, and not a character failing. If you are not recognising yourself, please tell someone. Intrusive frightening thoughts are more common than most women are warned, and they are a reason to seek support rather than to feel ashamed.

The fourth trimester

Recovery from birth is measured in seasons, not weeks. Hair shedding at three to four months is hormonal and self-limiting. Cycles may return at six weeks or at eighteen months. Both are normal. The only urgent question is whether you are being adequately supported, and it is a fair one to ask out loud.

Chapter Fourteen

Perimenopause

Not a sudden ending. A transition that commonly lasts four to eight years, and often begins earlier than women are told.

52
Chapter Fourteen · Perimenopause

Perimenopause typically begins in the early to mid forties, sometimes in the late thirties, and it does not announce itself. The defining feature is not falling oestrogen. It is fluctuation.

As the follicle pool dwindles, the brain compensates by raising FSH to push the remaining follicles harder. Some cycles respond with unusually high oestrogen peaks, higher than in your thirties. Others produce very little. Meanwhile ovulation becomes less reliable, and because progesterone is made only after ovulation, progesterone falls first and falls hardest.

That combination, erratic oestrogen with declining progesterone, explains most of the symptom picture: new or worsening anxiety, sleep that breaks around three in the morning, heavier or unpredictable bleeding, breast tenderness, migraines, and mood changes that feel unlike you. Many women are told they are simply stressed.

Metabolism shifts too. Insulin sensitivity declines, muscle mass falls faster without resistance training, and body composition changes even when weight does not.

Why testing often disappoints

A single FSH or oestradiol result in perimenopause is close to uninformative, because the whole point is that levels swing week to week. A normal result does not rule perimenopause out.

Diagnosis is usually clinical: your age, your symptoms, and the pattern of your cycles. If a clinician tells you your bloods were fine so this cannot be perimenopause, that is worth questioning.

What tends to help

Resistance training becomes non-negotiable rather than optional, for muscle, bone, metabolic health and mood.
Protein requirements rise as the body becomes less efficient at using it.
Sleep needs active protection, because it is often the first thing to go and the thing that makes everything else worse.
Alcohol tolerance frequently drops, and it worsens both hot flushes and sleep.
Menopausal hormone therapy is an evidence-based option for many women, and the picture is considerably more favourable than the coverage of two decades ago suggested. It is a genuine conversation to have with a well-informed clinician, weighing your own history. Neither dismissal nor evangelism serves you here.

Chapter Fifteen

Menopause and the Years Beyond

A single day, defined in retrospect, followed by a third of your life.

54
Chapter Fifteen · Menopause

Menopause itself is a point in time: twelve consecutive months without a period. The average age is around fifty-one. Everything after it is postmenopause, and for most women that is three or four decades.

Oestrogen does not vanish entirely. Production shifts to smaller amounts made in fat and other tissues from adrenal precursors. But the ovarian supply ends, and because oestrogen receptors exist throughout the body, the effects reach well beyond the reproductive system.

Bone loses density fastest in the first five to ten years after menopause; this is the window where weight-bearing and resistance exercise matters most. Cardiovascular risk rises as the protective effect of oestrogen ends. Genitourinary symptoms such as vaginal dryness, discomfort with sex and recurrent urinary infections affect a large proportion of women, worsen over time rather than settling, and are the single most under-treated aspect of menopause. Local vaginal oestrogen is highly effective, very low risk, and available to most women including many who cannot take systemic therapy.

Cognitive changes, particularly word-finding and concentration, are common during the transition and generally improve afterwards. They are not early dementia, and being told so plainly is often a relief.

The priorities, in order

Strength training two or three times weekly, for bone, muscle and metabolic health.
Protein at every meal, spread through the day.
Balance work, because falls are what turn thinning bone into a fracture.
Blood pressure, lipids and glucose checked on a schedule.
Sleep and stress, still, always.

Things you should not have to endure

Painful sex.
Recurrent urinary infections.
Sleep destroyed by night sweats.
Mood changes that alter your life.

Each of these has effective treatment. Being told it is simply your age is not a clinical answer.

The reframe

The years after menopause are not a decline to be managed. They are the longest single stage of a woman’s hormonal life, and what you build in the decade around the transition, in muscle, in bone, in metabolic health, is what you will be living on for the thirty years that follow.

Chapter Sixteen · For Men

Chapter Sixteen

A Short Chapter for Men

This book is about the female body. But hormonal health is rarely a solo project, and half of fertility is male.

Male hormonal physiology is steadier than female. Testosterone follows a daily rhythm, highest in the morning, rather than a monthly one, which is why a testosterone test should be taken early in the day and repeated before conclusions are drawn.

Levels decline gradually from around the age of thirty to forty, typically by about one percent a year. This is normal ageing, not a disorder, and it is quite different from the marketing around it. Genuinely low testosterone with symptoms deserves proper assessment; a single borderline result does not warrant treatment.

The lifestyle drivers are unsurprising and they are the same ones in this book. Poor sleep lowers testosterone quickly and substantially. Excess visceral fat increases conversion of testosterone to oestrogen. Chronic stress, excessive alcohol, sedentary living and under-recovery all lower it. Resistance training and adequate sleep raise it more reliably than any supplement sold for the purpose.

On fertility: sperm take around three months to develop, so changes made now show up a season later, exactly as with eggs. Heat, smoking, alcohol, some medications and obesity all affect quality. A semen analysis is inexpensive, non-invasive and quick, and it should be one of the first investigations when a couple is struggling, not something arranged after a year of testing the woman.

The point of this chapter

If you are reading this book to understand a partner, the most useful thing you can do is not learn her cycle. It is to recognise that the foundations are shared: sleep, food, movement, stress and alcohol shape both of your hormonal systems, and it is easier to change any of them together than alone.

Part Three · Testing

What to Test, and When

In women, timing is not a detail. The same blood drawn a week apart tells two different stories.

Time these to your cycle

Progesterone: about seven days before your period is due, not day 21 unless your cycle is exactly 28 days. This is the test that confirms ovulation.

FSH, LH, oestradiol: days two to five of the cycle, counting from the first day of proper bleeding.

Androgens (testosterone, free testosterone, SHBG, DHEA-S): morning, and in the early part of the cycle where possible.

These can be taken any day

AMH: a marker of remaining follicle pool. It does not predict your chance of conceiving naturally this month, and it is widely over-interpreted.

Thyroid: TSH, free T4, free T3, TPO antibodies.

Prolactin: morning, rested, avoiding exercise, sex or breast examination beforehand.

Metabolic: fasting insulin, HbA1c, glucose, lipids.

Nutritional: ferritin, vitamin D, B12.

Four things worth knowing before you test

Hormonal contraception changes almost everything above. Most sex hormone results are uninterpretable while taking it, because it suppresses your own production by design. Say so when tests are arranged.

“Normal” is a wide range built from a population. A result at the very edge of normal, in someone with clear symptoms, deserves discussion rather than dismissal.

One result is a snapshot. Especially in perimenopause, where the defining feature is variability. Patterns beat single points.

Test to answer a question. Broad private panels generate incidental findings that cause anxiety and rarely change management. Decide what you are asking first.

Chapter Seventeen

The Daily Foundations

Everything in this book eventually returns to five things. They are unglamorous, and they are what actually works.

64
Chapter Seventeen · Foundations

The five

1Sleep, protected properly. Hormonal regulation is largely nocturnal. A consistent wake time, morning light, and dimmed evenings do more for your cycle than any supplement in the cupboard.
2Enough food, eaten regularly. Under-eating is read by the hypothalamus as scarcity, and scarcity switches off reproduction. Protein at each meal, fibre in quantity, and thirty different plants across a week covers most of what matters.
3Movement that includes resistance. Muscle is the largest site of glucose disposal and the main defence against the metabolic shifts of midlife. Two or three sessions a week, plus daily walking, is the whole prescription.
4Stress that resolves. Not a life without pressure, but a nervous system that returns to baseline. Two minutes of slow breathing daily, time outdoors, and real conversation are the cheapest interventions available.
5Alcohol kept modest. It raises circulating oestrogen, competes for the liver capacity that clears it, fragments sleep and worsens hot flushes. It is the single easiest lever most women have not pulled.

What to expect, and when

Because a follicle matures over roughly ninety days, give any change three full cycles before judging it. Sleep and energy often shift within weeks. Cycle regularity and skin take months. Anything promising results in a fortnight is selling you something.

On supplements

Test before supplementing rather than after. The ones with genuine evidence in specific circumstances are few: vitamin D where deficient, iron where ferritin is low, folate before conception, myo-inositol in PCOS, magnesium for sleep and cramps. Most of the rest is expensive urine.

The one instruction

Choose one. Not five. Whichever of the foundations above you already know is your weakest, take that one and give it four weeks of honest attention. The others will still be here.

Closing

What You Take With You

If you remember nothing else from these pages, remember this: your cycle is a vital sign. It is a monthly report on whether your body currently believes it is well-fed, well-rested and safe enough to do the most expensive thing it knows how to do. When it changes, it is telling you something about the whole system, not just the ovaries.

Remember that no hormone acts alone. That the thyroid, the gut, the pancreas and the stress response all have a vote. That the most useful question is never which hormone is broken, but where in the conversation the signal was lost.

Remember that timing changes meaning. That a result is only interpretable if someone knows where you were in your cycle when it was taken, and that you are entitled to ask.

Remember that severe pain, absent periods and disabling mood changes are information, not weakness, and that being dismissed is a reason to ask again rather than to go quiet.

And remember that the eggs you carry were formed while your mother was carrying you. Whatever else this body has been, it has been continuous, and patient, and doing this all along without being asked.

Your next step

Turn the page. Track one full cycle. Not to optimise anything, and not to find something wrong, but because three months of your own data will tell you more about your body than anything else in this book.

You do not need to master your body. You only need to stop mistranslating it.
